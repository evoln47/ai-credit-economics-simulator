08

Measuring the demand inputs

The model's weakest inputs are the demand shape (20 / 200 / 1,500 actions a month) and the wall splits. Both are measurable before launch, and this is the plan I would run.

Demand shape: median, p90, p99 actions per user per month

Instrument every AI surface, not only the free one: Blueprint measures site builds well and the other four tasks badly, so builds are the calibration task and the task mix comes from the paid surfaces' event logs. Log four events per account: generation started, generation completed, credits that would have been charged under the §02 task table, and plan tier. Run the logging for a full quarter, not four weeks: building is episodic, so a short window censors exactly the heavy-then-idle users the tail describes, and monthly per-account counts need complete months or an explicit censoring adjustment for accounts observed partway through. Read the median, p90, and p99 of monthly actions per active account instead of assuming a lognormal, and test the lognormal fit against the observed tail. Sample-size rule: report a percentile only for tier cells with at least 1,000 active accounts, and treat the p99 from a cell that size as resting on roughly ten accounts above the line, which makes it a range rather than a point; the picker acts on the range's lower end. Decision this informs: the allowance percentile in §05, which today rests on an assumed shape.

Wall splits: pay, upgrade, downgrade, churn, stay

Run the shortfall policy as an experiment before it is policy. Give a cohort of accounts a soft wall (a warning at 80% of allowance, a hard stop at 100%, with a one-click top-up and a one-click upgrade shown side by side), hold a matched control group on the current policy, and record which of the five responses each wall user takes within 30 days. The five observed shares replace the §03 assumptions one for one, including the upgrade share, which has no published value anywhere. Guardrail: the wall cohort's departure rate is judged against the control group's, not against the 4.2% book-level baseline, because a wall-exposed cohort and an all-cause book rate have different denominators; the 4.2% figure is used only as a reasonableness bound on the blended book after the shares are adopted. Cell rule: five shares across four tiers is twenty cells, and a share is adopted only for cells with at least 500 wall users; smaller cells keep the assumption and say so. Decision this informs: every §03 slider, and whether the reconciliation cap in Methodology §08 still binds at observed rates.

Take-up and serving cost

Take-up is observed in the same experiment: the share of wall users who buy, and the share of the shortfall they buy, are two different numbers and the model keeps them separate (Methodology §08). Serving cost per action is reconciled monthly against provider invoices at the task level, which is also the check on the per-task routing shares in §02: if invoiced frontier tokens diverge from the modeled shares, the shares move, not the invoice. Decision this informs: the top-up price in §03 and the routing default in §01.

Documentation

Methodology

How the simulator works, where the defaults come from, and where it stops being useful. If this page and the code disagree, the code is right.

01

What the model does

One question: what does it cost to serve AI usage sold as plan credits, and which allowance and shortfall rules keep that cost inside a target margin? A pricing model, not a forecast: no data from Squarespace's actual business.

02

Inputs and defaults

Plans

Squarespace's published tiers at annual billing, checked October 2026. Basic is $19 a month with 10 credits granted once and no monthly refill. Core is $29 with 20 credits a month. Plus is $49 with 40 a month. Advanced is $99 with 120 a month. Prices in the model are monthly equivalents.

Provider rate card

Public list prices per 1M tokens, November 2026. The frontier model (Sonnet class) is $3 input and $15 output. The standard model (mini and Haiku class) is $0.30 input and $1.50 output. Image generation is a flat $0.04 per image. A blended text rate assumes 60% input tokens and 40% output tokens. The effective rate shown in §01 is the model's realized text rate: total text serving cost divided by total text tokens across the task mix, computed from the per-task costs rather than from a single blend. The §01 routing slider scales every task's share together, so at the 30% default the shares in the task table apply as entered. Image generation is flat per image and is excluded from that rate. A quality index reports the mix-weighted output quality next to cost: frontier output scores 100 and the standard model scores 85 by default, an assumption with no published benchmark behind it.

Tasks

Five tasks carry demand: site build, page regeneration, copy draft, image generation, and SEO scan. Each has a token count, a credit price, a share of monthly actions, and a frontier share. These are analyst estimates, not published figures, and they are the softest inputs in the model. The percentile guardrail users are also costed on a tail mix tilted toward site builds (30% build share by default, against 5% in the average mix); the tail-mix margin appears beside the p99 margin in §04 and as a row in the §05 picker, so the gap is visible where the guardrail is read.

Competitor benchmarks

The packaging notes come from Framer, Wix, and Webflow public pricing and help pages, checked in October and November 2026. They describe packaging only. They do not feed the simulation.

Behavior evidence

From the published-record review in §09 (Nov 2026): Antenna/Netflix via TechCrunch, Spotify's Q3 2023 call, ChartMogul via GrowPanel, Recurly 2023, Chargebee 2025, Grubb and Osborne (2015), Lambrecht and Skiera (2006). §09 tags mark how each figure was checked; index-grade figures rest on search text or secondary reproductions.

03

Usage distribution

Monthly actions follow a lognormal. The median slider sets the centre exactly; the spread (sigma) is fitted to p90 and p99 by least squares on the log scale (z-scores 1.2816 and 2.3263). One lognormal rarely passes through all three points, so the simulator prints fitted and slider values side by side.

Credit demand = action count × average credits per action; serving cost = action count × average cost per action. Actions are not drawn individually, so two users with the same count are costed identically.

04

Simulation design

Monte Carlo, fixed seed: a mulberry32 generator (seed 20261106) produces one fixed array of 60,000 standard normal draws, 10,000 users across six months, and that same array is reused for every tier, preset, and scenario. This is common random numbers: because the draws are shared, differences between scenarios are policy effects rather than sampling noise, and identical settings give identical figures. Each user runs six months so rollover banks settle; all figures are month six. Median, p90, and p99 costs come from the simulated users nearest those percentiles.

05

Policy mechanics

Hard cap

Actions stop when the credit balance runs out. Unserved demand costs nothing and earns nothing.

Overage billing

Full demand is served. Credits beyond the balance are billed at the plan's implied rate (price divided by monthly credits) times the overage multiple. Basic has no implied rate, so its overage is priced at the top-up price.

Top-up packs

Half of any shortfall is bought at the top-up price; half is forgone. The half is an assumption, and results are sensitive to it.

Rollover and breakage

Rollover banks unused credits up to one month of allowance; the rest expires. Basic's grant lands in month one only. Breakage = expired ÷ available credits in month six.

06

Margins and the picker

Margin per user per month: (revenue − serving cost) ÷ revenue, revenue being price plus overage/top-up revenue. A tier breaches when its margin, or its p99 user's, falls below the floor. Portfolio figures use the §06 mix weights, equal user counts by default. The picker swaps one plan's allowance for a demand percentile, price and policy held.

07

Decomposition

The bridge is read in a fixed order: volume first (action counts), then mix (the routing share between models at unchanged per-route prices), then rate (provider prices). Each effect is a single counterfactual measured against the same base, and all three are then applied together. Interaction is the residual left after the three single effects are subtracted from the total change. It exists because the shocks were measured separately and in that order, so it is a property of the decomposition rather than a separate driver to act on. Under a hard cap the volume effect is near zero by construction: cost is bounded by allowances.

08

Buyer behavior

The margin simulation holds the population fixed at 10,000 users per plan. The behavior layer lets it move, in month six only, and produces its own adjusted revenue and margin alongside the static figures: it does not feed back into §04.

Defaults come from published evidence (§09, checked Nov 2026). No traceable SaaS price-elasticity coefficient exists from any named firm, and Squarespace publishes no wall-reaction rates, so the defaults combine the closest citable evidence: consumer price events, billing-platform churn levels, a survey downgrade ratio, and an academic finding on too-small plans. Paid take-up has no published value and stays an explicit assumption.

Price response

Each plan's share shifts by its relative price change raised to the elasticity (default −0.1), derived from Netflix's January 2022 increase (Antenna: churn 3.0% in the increase month, up 0.95 pts from a 2.0% 2021 average). Spotify's 2023 increase produced no detectable change (bound: 0 to −0.1); Netflix 2011 gives about −0.07. All are $10–$20 consumer products with near-zero switching costs, so −0.1 reads as an upper bound for a product hosting a business's site and domain. About a third of the response leaves the product entirely; the rest switches plans. That split is a modeling choice with no published source.

The wall

The wall-hit share comes from the simulation itself; Grubb and Osborne (2015), about 20% of users on $35–$55 cellular plans incurred overages, is the published check. Wall users split five ways in this layer: pay (where a paid policy exists), upgrade one plan, downgrade one plan, churn, or stay, and the five shares always sum to the wall population; under a hard cap there is nothing to buy, so the pay share is zero and those users stay. Basic's covered share is 0% by construction, since it carries no monthly allowance, so its month-six exits are arithmetic rather than evidence, and inbound downgrades refill it in the ending mix. Squarespace's base bills mostly annually, so for annual subscribers read month-six movement as renewal risk at month twelve, not mid-term cancellation. Wall churn defaults to 13% under a hard cap: ChartMogul's 4.2% baseline monthly churn at $25–$100, times a conservative 3× stand-in for Lambrecht and Skiera's 4.4–11.4× range for too-small plans. Wall downgrade defaults to 8%, from Chargebee's 2025 downgrade-to-cancel ratio (14% downgraded, 22% cancelled). The upgrade share defaults to 15% and, like paid take-up, has no published value; it is an assumption, exposed as a slider with the same status. Paid policies scale the responses down (churn 40%/55%, downgrades 50%/60%, upgrades 70% for overage/top-ups): modeling assumptions with no published source. Basic downgraders count as churn.

Reconciliation. The cap is tested on a raw total: price-response departures plus every wall user's churn share, with Basic's downgrades counted inside that total because the page counts them as churn. When the raw total exceeds twice the 4.2% all-cause baseline (8.4% of the starting population, 3,360 users at the default population), the wall churn share scales down so the test total meets the cap, and the behavior card reports the scale factor. One consequence is stated plainly: Basic's downgrades are counted in the test total but are not themselves scaled, so realized churn lands above the cap figure (3,550 users, 8.9%, at defaults against the 3,360 cap). The cap is therefore a cap on the test total, not a guarantee on realized churn, and the difference is the unscaled Basic downgrade flow. Downgraders are not re-tested against the lower plan's wall (see Limits); nothing in this layer re-tests anyone. The cap is a discipline rule, not an estimate: it stops the behavior layer from implying a departure rate the baseline evidence cannot support, at the cost of muting the wall response exactly where allowances bite hardest. Before this rebuild the wall alone produced about 11% monthly departures at defaults with no reconciliation at all.

Paid take-up

Take-up, the share of wall users who pay, defaults to 20%, an assumption inside a 10–25% sensitivity range; no published value exists for any SaaS product. Zero under a hard cap. It is a different quantity from the share of a shortfall bought inside the simulation (half, under the top-up policy; §05), and the two should not be read as one number. The behavior layer restates overage/top-up revenue at this rate; serving cost per remaining user is not restated (see Limits).

Upgrades at the wall

The upgrade share, the proportion of wall users who move up one plan instead of paying, leaving, or staying, defaults to 15% under a hard cap and 70% of that under the paid policies. Like take-up it has no published value for any SaaS product; it is an assumption, exposed as a slider in §03, and the five wall shares are normalized so they always sum to the wall population.

Adjusted totals

Leavers take their revenue and cost; downgraders count at the lower plan's price and cost. Adjusted margin = (adjusted revenue − ending population's cost) ÷ adjusted revenue.

Wix reported about 6.3 million premium subscriptions against 263 million registered users for fiscal 2023, which works out to a cumulative free-to-paid conversion of about 2.4%, a figure the review computed from the two reported numbers because Wix does not publish the rate. Creative Subscriptions revenue was $1.15 billion, 74% of the total, and Business Solutions was $409.7 million, 26%, including transaction revenue. Total revenue over premium subscriptions works out to about $248 a year, roughly $21 a month per premium subscription including transaction revenue. Source: Wix fiscal 2023 results, February 21, 2024. nasdaq.com Verified live

Two consumer analogues bound the shape of a tier mix. After Netflix phased out its mid-priced ad-free Basic tier for new and rejoining members, its cheapest ads tier grew about 70% quarter over quarter for two consecutive quarters and took 40% of all new sign-ups in ads markets, according to the Q4 2023 shareholder letter. That shift was engineered by removing a tier rather than by a pure price change. Source: sec.gov Verified live At Spotify in Q3 2023, 226 million Premium subscribers against 574 million total monthly users means 39% of users pay and 61% stay free after a decade of freemium operation.

The category's current price ladders, taken from October 2026 checked-pricing compilations, clear the market inside the same band this simulator models: Squarespace at $19, $29, $49, and $99 a month billed annually and $25 to $139 billed monthly; Wix at $17, $29, $39, and $159; Shopify at $29, $79, and $299 with Plus from $2,300; and GoDaddy entry pricing at about $9.99. A live check of Squarespace's own pricing page was attempted during the review and the browser failed, so these ladder figures are index-grade. Sources: Rankite and Wix. Index

Outside options bound willingness to pay from above. A do-it-yourself builder build runs $0 to $450 on Forbes Advisor's figures, a freelancer-built basic site runs about $6,760 all-in for year one on FreshBooks' breakdown, and most agency projects reviewed on Clutch cost under $10,000, all as compiled in Zarla's 2026 cost guide. A $19–$99 monthly plan, $228 to $1,188 a year, sits an order of magnitude below the freelancer and agency alternatives, which is consistent with the low price sensitivity observed inside this band, although no published study converts that relationship into a sensitivity coefficient. Source: Zarla. Index

On billing period, the modal annual discount across more than 1,000 SaaS businesses in Recurly's plan-structure study from January 2017 to July 2018 was 16.7%, or two months free, while Squarespace's own ladder embeds an annual discount of about 24% at Basic. Recurly's 2025 State of Subscriptions reports that annual plans generate 50–60% more revenue per user than monthly plans, with early monthly churn as the driver. Source: Recurly. Index

Allowance and overage behavior, from the academic record

The strongest evidence comes from metered telecom and internet tariffs, not SaaS. Its direction: the risk sits with users on too-small plans, and it shows up as churn rather than willing overage payment.

Grubb and Osborne (2015), in the American Economic Review, studied US cellular bills from 2002 to 2004 and found that about 20% of users on $35–$55 monthly plans incurred overage fees of about $20 a month. Across the sample period, 19% of subscribers incurred an overage above $100, 8% above $200, and 3% above $300. Consumers were overconfident about how much their usage would vary, that overconfidence cost them $76 a year at 2002–04 prices, and users who received usage alerts reduced their usage significantly. The one-in-five incidence rate is the best published anchor for the share of allowance-plan users who hit a cap in a period. Sources: American Economic Association and the University of Toronto summary. Verified live

An FCC consumer survey from 2010 with 3,005 US adults found that 17% of mobile users, about one in six or roughly 30 million people, had experienced a sudden bill increase that was not caused by a plan change, and 84% of them said their carrier did not contact them as they approached or exceeded their allowance. More than a third of those users saw a jump of at least $50 and 23% saw at least $100. A GAO figure from 2009 cited in the same proceeding put unexpected charges at 34% of wireless subscribers. Source: FCC. Verified live

Lambrecht and Skiera (2006), in the Journal of Marketing Research, examined about 10,882 internet-access bills over five months and found that up to 46.4% of customers showed flat-rate bias, choosing a tariff that was too large, against at most 5.8% who chose one that was too small. More than half of the flat-rate-biased group paid at least 100% more than the cheapest available tariff would have cost them. Customers on too-large plans did not churn more and carried 87–135% higher lifetime value, while customers on too-small plans, the group that hits caps and pays overages, churned at monthly rates 340–1,040% higher and carried slightly negative lifetime value. A related literature summary puts the share of flat-rate internet subscribers who would have saved money on pay-per-use at about 48%. Source: literature summary in the Journal of Revenue and Pricing Management. Index

DellaVigna and Malmendier (2006), in the American Economic Review, studied 7,752 US health-club members and found that members on flat monthly contracts above $70 attended 4.3 times a month and paid more than $17 per visit when a $10 per-visit pass existed, about 70% more per visit. 80% of monthly members would have saved money paying per visit, and average forgone savings ran to about $600 per membership. Buyers over-buy allowance because they choose for their expected peak usage rather than their average usage. Index

Two smaller findings complete the picture. Spotify Premium includes 15 hours a month of audiobooks with paid top-ups beyond that allowance, and management said on the Q3 2023 call that some users were already buying extra hours on the first day while describing the base as very small, with no take-up rate disclosed. In Chargebee's 2025 survey, 70% of consumers said they are open to usage-based pricing, which records an attitude rather than behavior at a cap. Index

Open questions. The published record (Nov 2026) does not answer these, and the simulator does not pretend otherwise.
  • No SaaS price-elasticity coefficient from any named firm, and no academic estimate for subscription software, was found. Aggregator figures could not be traced and were rejected.
  • No subscriber counts by plan tier, and no published tier-mix shift after a price or allowance change, exist for any builder.
  • No published split of cap-hitters into pay / upgrade / reduce / churn exists for any metered product.
  • No published willingness-to-pay study exists for website builders or SMB online-presence software.
  • Superhuman's figures rest on one company-reported account; its 4% variant is ambiguous between total and incremental churn.
  • Builder price ladders are index-grade: the live check of Squarespace's pricing page failed during the review.

Assumptions

  • Task token counts, credit prices, and mix shares are estimates set by the analyst.
  • Text tasks split 60% input tokens and 40% output tokens.
  • An abandoned build costs the same as a kept build. No refund and no partial credit.
  • Top-up buyers cover exactly half of any shortfall.
  • In the margin simulation, users keep their plan, do not churn, and do not time usage to the billing cycle. Movement between plans is estimated separately, in the behavior layer.
  • Behavior defaults from published evidence where it exists (§09): elasticity −0.1, wall churn 13%, wall downgrades 8%. Paid take-up (20%), the paid-policy scalings, and the one-third exit share are analyst assumptions.
  • Users on all four plans share one usage distribution.
  • Prices are list prices. No negotiated rates, prompt caching, or batch discounts.
  • All AI cost is charged against the full plan price, including the part of the price that pays for the website itself.
11

Limits

  • Overage revenue is an upper bound: it bills the full simulated shortfall. Top-up mode bills half of any shortfall, the half users are assumed to buy; the other half is forgone. Real heavy users upgrade, churn, or stop before paying several times the plan price.
  • Behavior defaults come from adjacent products, not this one: elasticity from $10–$20 entertainment subscriptions (far lower switching costs than a business website, so likely overstated here), wall churn from internet tariffs. Outputs show what follows from the rates chosen, not what will happen.
  • Paid take-up has no published value, and no published cap-hit split exists. Answers depending on the take-up slider rest on an unsettled assumption.
  • The equal 10,000-per-plan starting mix is a convention, not an observed mix: §09 filings show revenue concentrating in lower plan families.
  • Movement happens once, in month six. Downgraders are not re-tested against the lower plan's wall, and nobody is won back.
  • One elasticity serves all four plans; competitor pull-in is not modelled.
  • Take-up restates revenue only. Serving cost per remaining user is held at the simulated figure, so adjusted margins under the paid policies are approximate. The take-up assumption itself is large: at the baseline plan settings it moves adjusted revenue per starting user by $48.00 in top-up mode and $83.03 in overage mode across the 10% to 25% sensitivity range. The unrestated-cost asymmetry is the smaller effect inside that swing, because serving cost per user is an order of magnitude below plan prices in this model, but it is not quantified separately and the adjusted margins should be read as approximate for that reason.
  • One distribution serves all four tiers. Higher tiers attract heavier users in practice, so costs at the top are likely understated and costs at the bottom overstated.
  • Mix averages hide variation. Users with the same action count are costed identically.
  • There is no demand response. A larger allowance does not change usage in the model. In a product, it would.
  • The inputs are snapshots. Plan prices and provider rates date from October and November 2026 and will drift.
  • The margin simulation says nothing about willingness to pay, conversion, or churn; it covers cost and margin only. Movement is estimated separately in the behavior layer, on the evidence in §09, and inherits that evidence's limits.
  • The behavior layer's wall split is reconciled by construction: the raw test total (price departures plus wall churn, Basic downgrades counted) is capped at twice the 4.2% baseline churn, and wall churn scales down to fit when the raw responses exceed the cap. Realized departures run above the cap figure because Basic downgrades are counted in the test but not scaled. Read adjusted figures as bounded by that discipline rule, not as a forecast of actual departures.
  • Upgraders are costed at the average cost of the tier they join, though they are the heaviest users of the tier they left. The upgrade flow's cost side is therefore understated and the adjusted margin flattered by an amount the model does not separately quantify.
  • Image generation scores 100 on the quality index by construction: it is a flat per-image cost with no routing decision, so a fifth of the action mix sits at frontier quality in the index regardless of the routing settings.
