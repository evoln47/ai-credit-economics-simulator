AI Credit Economics Simulator

Squarespace sells AI as plan credits. This model asks what those credits cost to serve, and which allowance and shortfall rules keep that cost inside a target margin when, at the baseline, the median user needs about 3.6 times the Core allowance. 10,000 users per plan, month 6 reported. Every margin in the model is an AI serving margin: plan revenue minus the cost of serving that plan's AI usage, with the rest of Squarespace's cost base outside the model.

Assumptions

  • Demand follows a lognormal curve fitted to three analyst-set anchors: a median of 20 actions per user per month, a 90th percentile of 200, and a 99th percentile of 1,500. No published usage data sits behind these anchors.
  • The simulation runs 40,000 users, 10,000 per plan, sorted into plan bands by demand level, over six months. Month six is the reported month.
  • A user's demand level persists from month to month (correlation 0.7). The rest of each month's demand is fresh noise.
  • Token costs blend input and output prices at 60/40. Heavy users run a tail mix with builds at 30% of actions, against 5% in the average mix.
  • 20% of users who hit their allowance wall take the paid option. This take-up rate is an assumption with no published value behind it.
  • Baseline churn is 4.2% a month, and total departures in any scenario are capped at twice that baseline.
  • A top-up purchase serves half of a user's shortfall.
  • Every margin is an AI serving margin: plan revenue minus the cost of serving that plan's AI usage. Hosting, support, research, sales, and the rest of the cost base sit outside the model, so these margins run higher than a company gross margin and compare policies against each other.
  • Every run uses a fixed random seed, so the same settings always produce the same draws.

08

Measuring the demand inputs

The model's weakest inputs are the demand shape (20 / 200 / 1,500 actions a month) and the wall splits. Both are measurable before launch, and this is the plan I would run.

Demand shape: median, p90, p99 actions per user per month

Instrument every AI surface, not only the free one: A surface that is never logged cannot be priced, so the logging covers all five tasks: builds are the calibration task because the onboarding surface is the one a launch instruments first, and the task mix comes from the paid surfaces' event logs. Log four events per account: generation started, generation completed, credits that would have been charged under the §02 task table, and plan tier. Run the logging for a full quarter, not four weeks: building is episodic, so a short window censors exactly the heavy-then-idle users the tail describes, and monthly per-account counts need complete months or an explicit censoring adjustment for accounts observed partway through. Read the median, p90, and p99 of monthly actions per active account instead of assuming a lognormal, and test the lognormal fit against the observed tail. Sample-size rule: report a percentile only for tier cells with at least 1,000 active accounts, and treat the p99 from a cell that size as resting on roughly ten accounts above the line, which makes it a range rather than a point; the picker acts on the range's lower end. Decision this informs: the allowance percentile in §05, which today rests on an assumed shape.

Wall splits: pay, upgrade, downgrade, churn, stay

Run the shortfall policy as an experiment before it is policy. Give a cohort of accounts a soft wall (a warning at 80% of allowance, a hard stop at 100%, with a one-click top-up and a one-click upgrade shown side by side), hold a matched control group on the current policy, and record which of the five responses each wall user takes within 30 days. The five observed shares replace the §03 assumptions one for one for the wall design tested, including the upgrade share, which has no published value anywhere; the splits under the other shortfall policies keep the scaling assumptions in Methodology §08 until each policy runs in its own cell. Guardrail: the wall cohort's departure rate is judged against the control group's, not against the 4.2% book-level baseline, because a wall-exposed cohort and an all-cause book rate have different denominators; the 4.2% figure is used only as a reasonableness bound on the blended book after the shares are adopted. Cell rule: five shares across four tiers is twenty cells, and a share is adopted only for cells with at least 500 wall users; smaller cells keep the assumption and say so. Decision this informs: every §03 slider, and whether the reconciliation cap in Methodology §08 still binds at observed rates.

Take-up and serving cost

Take-up is observed in the same experiment: the share of wall users who buy, and the share of the shortfall they buy, are two different numbers and the model keeps them separate (Methodology §08). Serving cost per action is reconciled monthly against provider invoices at the task level, which is also the check on the per-task routing shares in §02: if invoiced frontier tokens diverge from the modeled shares, the shares move, not the invoice. Decision this informs: the top-up price in §03 and the routing default in §01.

Documentation

Methodology

How the simulator works, where the defaults come from, and where it stops being useful. If this page and the code disagree, the code is right.

01

What the model does

One question: what does it cost to serve AI usage sold as plan credits, and which allowance and shortfall rules keep that cost inside a target margin? A pricing model, not a forecast: no data from Squarespace's actual business. It takes in the published plan ladder and credit allowances, a per-task table of credit prices and token counts priced at provider list rates, and a demand distribution fitted to three analyst-set anchors. It simulates 40,000 users over six months, sorted into the four plan tiers by demand, with the chosen shortfall policy and buyer behavior applied each month. It reports the serving margin by tier, the allowance implied by a chosen percentile of a tier's own demand, and a decomposition of what moves the result.

02

Inputs and defaults

Plans

Squarespace's published tiers at annual billing, from October 2026 pricing compilations. The review's direct check of Squarespace's own pricing page failed, so the ladder figures are index-grade, as §09 records. Basic is $19 a month with 10 credits granted once and no monthly refill. Core is $29 with 20 credits a month. Plus is $49 with 40 a month. Advanced is $99 with 120 a month. Prices in the model are monthly equivalents.

Provider rate card

Public list prices per 1M tokens, November 2026. The frontier model (Sonnet class) is $3 input and $15 output. The standard model (mini and Haiku class) is $0.30 input and $1.50 output. Image generation is a flat $0.04 per image. A blended text rate assumes 60% input tokens and 40% output tokens. The effective rate shown in §01 is the model's realized text rate: total text serving cost divided by total text tokens across the task mix, computed from the per-task costs rather than from a single blend. The §01 routing slider scales every task's share together, so at the 30% default the shares in the task table apply as entered. Image generation is flat per image and is excluded from that rate. The two classes are composites across providers' public pricing pages, not one vendor's card. A quality index reports the mix-weighted output quality next to cost: frontier output scores 100 and the standard model scores 85 by default, an assumption with no published benchmark behind it.

Tasks

Demand comes from five tasks: site build, page regeneration, copy draft, image generation, and SEO scan. Each has a token count, a credit price, a share of monthly actions, and a frontier share. These are analyst estimates, not published figures, and they are the softest inputs in the model. The percentile guardrail users are also costed on a tail mix tilted toward site builds (30% build share by default, against 5% in the average mix); the tail-mix margin appears beside the p99 margin in §04 and as a row in the §05 picker, so the gap is visible where the guardrail is read.

Competitor benchmarks

The packaging notes come from Framer, Wix, and Webflow public pricing and help pages, checked in October and November 2026. They describe packaging only. They do not feed the simulation.

Behavior evidence

From the published-record review in §09 (Nov 2026): Antenna/Netflix via TechCrunch, Spotify's Q3 2023 call, ChartMogul via GrowPanel, Recurly 2023, Chargebee 2025, Grubb and Osborne (2015), Lambrecht and Skiera (2006). §09 tags mark how each figure was checked; index-grade figures rest on search text or secondary reproductions.

03

Usage distribution

Monthly actions follow a lognormal. The median slider sets the centre exactly; the spread (sigma) is fitted to p90 and p99 by least squares on the log scale (z-scores 1.2816 and 2.3263). One lognormal rarely passes through all three points, so the simulator prints fitted and slider values side by side.

Credit demand = action count × average credits per action; serving cost = action count × average cost per action. Actions are not drawn individually, so two users with the same count are costed identically.

The pooled distribution is a mixture of four tier bands. Users sort into plans by demand level (§04), so each plan's users form one band of the pooled shape, with its own median and tail. §02 prints each band's observed median, p90, and p99 beside the pooled histogram.

04

Simulation design

Monte Carlo, fixed seed: a mulberry32 generator (seed 20261106) produces two fixed arrays of standard normal draws: a persistent demand level for each of 40,000 population positions, and an independent monthly noise draw for each position and month (240,000 draws). A month's demand draw for a position combines the two, with 70% of the variance coming from the persistent level and 30% from that month's noise. The 0.7 split is an analyst assumption with no published value. Because the combination is still standard normal, the pooled month-six distribution is the same fitted lognormal as before; what changes is who sits where. Positions are sorted once by their persistent level and split into quarters: the lightest 10,000 start on Basic, the next 10,000 on Core, the next on Plus, and the heaviest 10,000 on Advanced. Each tier is then simulated on its own band through the six-month mechanics, so rollover banks settle per user type: a persistently heavy user banks little and meets the wall steadily, where independent monthly draws would let the same position be light one month and heavy the next. The assignment is computed once from the fixed draws and is shared by every preset and scenario. This is common random numbers: because the population and the assignment are shared, differences between scenarios are policy effects rather than sampling noise, and identical settings give identical figures. All figures are month six. Median, p90, and p99 costs come from the simulated users nearest those percentiles inside each tier's own band. The design's main limitation is that the sorting is perfect and the quarters are equal: real plan choice also reflects price sensitivity, team size, and habit, so real bands overlap more than these do, and the equal split is a modeling choice rather than Squarespace's book (§09).

05

Policy mechanics

Hard cap

Actions stop when the credit balance runs out. Unserved demand costs nothing and earns nothing.

Overage billing

Full demand is served. Credits beyond the balance are billed at the plan's implied rate (price divided by monthly credits) times the overage multiple. A plan with no monthly credits has no implied rate, so its overage is priced at the top-up price times the overage multiple. Basic is that case at the published defaults.

Top-up packs

Half of any shortfall is bought at the top-up price; half is forgone. The half is an assumption, and results are sensitive to it.

Rollover and breakage

Rollover banks unused credits up to one month of allowance; the rest expires. Basic's grant lands in month one only. Breakage = expired ÷ available credits in month six.

One pool per user

The simulation tracks one credit total and one cost total per user. When a shortfall binds, served cost scales down in proportion to the credit shortfall across every task, including any task priced at zero credits. A zero-credit task is therefore not served in full for a user past the allowance inside this model; its cost leaves in the same proportion. The presets that zero out a task's price (Builds unmetered, Metered today) inherit this simplification, and their serving-cost figures should be read with it in mind.

06

Margins and the picker

Margin per user per month: (revenue − serving cost) ÷ revenue, revenue being price plus overage/top-up revenue. A tier breaches when its margin, or its p99 user's, falls below the floor. Portfolio figures use the §06 mix weights, equal user counts by default. The picker swaps one plan's allowance for a percentile of that plan's own tier band, price and policy held. In overage mode the plan's current implied rate is held as well, so the comparison isolates the allowance instead of repricing overage through the allowance in the rate's denominator.

07

Decomposition

The bridge is read in a fixed order: volume first (action counts), then mix (the routing share between models at unchanged per-route prices), then rate (provider prices). Each effect is a single counterfactual measured against the same base, and all three are then applied together. Interaction is the residual left after the three single effects are subtracted from the total change. It exists because the shocks were measured separately, so it is a property of the decomposition rather than a separate driver to act on. Under a hard cap the volume effect is near zero by construction: cost is bounded by allowances.

08

Buyer behavior

The margin simulation holds the population fixed at 10,000 users per plan. The behavior layer lets it move, in month six only, and produces its own adjusted revenue and margin alongside the static figures: it does not feed back into §04.

Defaults come from published evidence (§09, checked Nov 2026). No traceable SaaS price-elasticity coefficient exists from any named firm, and Squarespace publishes no wall-reaction rates, so the defaults combine the closest citable evidence: consumer price events, billing-platform churn levels, a survey downgrade ratio, and an academic finding on too-small plans. Paid take-up has no published value and stays an explicit assumption.

Price response

Each plan's share shifts by its relative price change raised to the elasticity (default −0.1), derived from Netflix's January 2022 increase (Antenna: churn 3.0% in the increase month, up 0.95 pts from a 2.0% 2021 average). Spotify's 2023 increase produced no detectable change (bound: 0 to −0.1); Netflix 2011 gives about −0.07. All are $10–$20 consumer products with near-zero switching costs, so −0.1 reads as an upper bound for a product hosting a business's site and domain. Each plan's price enters as a ratio to its published baseline price in §01, and the total population response is damped by a 0.35 exponent on the average of those ratios; the rest of the response redistributes share between plans. The 0.35 damper is a modeling choice with no published source.

The wall

The wall-hit share comes from the simulation itself; Grubb and Osborne (2015), about 20% of users on $35–$55 cellular plans incurred overages, is the published check. Wall users split five ways in this layer: pay (where a paid policy exists), upgrade one plan, downgrade one plan, churn, or stay, and the five shares always sum to the wall population; under a hard cap there is nothing to buy, so the pay share is zero and those users stay. At the published defaults Basic's covered share is 0% by construction, since it has no monthly allowance there, so its month-six exits are arithmetic rather than evidence, and inbound downgrades refill it in the ending mix. Giving Basic a monthly allowance in the simulator changes that share. For annual subscribers, read month-six movement as renewal risk at month twelve, not mid-term cancellation. Wall churn defaults to 13% under a hard cap: ChartMogul's 4.2% baseline monthly churn at $25–$100, times a conservative 3× stand-in for Lambrecht and Skiera's 4.4–11.4× range for too-small plans. Wall downgrade defaults to 8%, from Chargebee's 2025 downgrade-to-cancel ratio (14% downgraded, 22% cancelled). The upgrade share defaults to 15% and, like paid take-up, has no published value; it is an assumption, exposed as a slider with the same status. Paid policies scale the responses down (churn 40%/55%, downgrades 50%/60%, upgrades 70% for overage/top-ups): modeling assumptions with no published source. Basic downgraders count as churn.

Reconciliation. The cap is tested on a raw total: price-response departures plus every wall user's churn share, with Basic's downgrades counted inside that total because the page counts them as churn. When the raw total exceeds twice the 4.2% all-cause baseline (8.4% of the starting population, 3,360 users at the default population), the wall churn share scales down by a factor computed against that raw total, and the behavior card reports the scale factor. The share removed from churn moves into the stay share, so the five shares still sum to the wall population, and price departures enter the test floored at zero, so a net price inflow cannot enlarge the allowance. One consequence is stated plainly: Basic's downgrades are counted in the test total but are not themselves scaled, so realized churn lands above the cap figure (3,656 users, 9.1%, at defaults against the 3,360 cap). The cap is therefore a cap on the test total, not a guarantee on realized churn, and the difference is the unscaled Basic downgrade flow. Downgraders are not re-tested against the lower plan's wall (see Limits); nothing in this layer re-tests anyone. The cap is a discipline rule, not an estimate: it stops the behavior layer from implying a departure rate the baseline evidence cannot support, at the cost of muting the wall response exactly where allowances bite hardest. Before this rebuild the wall alone produced about 11% monthly departures at defaults with no reconciliation at all.

Paid take-up

Take-up, the share of wall users who pay, defaults to 20%, an assumption inside a 10–25% sensitivity range; no published value exists for any SaaS product. Zero under a hard cap. It is a different quantity from the share of a shortfall bought inside the simulation (half, under the top-up policy; §05), and the two should not be read as one number. The behavior layer restates overage and top-up revenue at this rate: overage revenue scales by the take-up share against full-shortfall billing, and top-up revenue scales by the take-up share at the policy's half-shortfall depth, the depth the simulation applies to every user. Serving cost per remaining user is not restated (see Limits).

Upgrades at the wall

The upgrade share, the proportion of wall users who move up one plan instead of paying, leaving, or staying, defaults to 15% under a hard cap and 70% of that under the paid policies. Like take-up it has no published value for any SaaS product; it is an assumption, exposed as a slider in §03, and the five wall shares are normalized so they always sum to the wall population.

Adjusted totals

Leavers take their revenue and cost; downgraders count at the lower plan's price and cost. Adjusted margin = (adjusted revenue − ending population's cost) ÷ adjusted revenue.

Because Commerce plans cost more, the Commerce share of subscribers sits below its 30.4% share of revenue. Allowance and overage behavior, from the academic record

The strongest evidence comes from metered telecom and internet tariffs, not SaaS. Its direction: the risk sits with users on too-small plans, and it shows up as churn rather than willing overage payment.

Grubb and Osborne (2015), in the American Economic Review, studied US cellular bills from 2002 to 2004 and found that about 20% of users on $35–$55 monthly plans incurred overage fees of about $20 a month. Across the sample period, 19% of subscribers incurred an overage above $100, 8% above $200, and 3% above $300. Consumers were overconfident about how much their usage would vary, that overconfidence cost them $76 a year at 2002–04 prices, and users who received usage alerts reduced their usage significantly. The one-in-five incidence rate is the best published anchor for the share of allowance-plan users who hit a cap in a period. Sources: American Economic Association and the University of Toronto summary. Verified live

An FCC consumer survey from 2010 with 3,005 US adults found that 17% of mobile users, about one in six or roughly 30 million people, had experienced a sudden bill increase that was not caused by a plan change, and 84% of them said their carrier did not contact them as they approached or exceeded their allowance. More than a third of those users saw a jump of at least $50 and 23% saw at least $100. A GAO figure from 2009 cited in the same proceeding put unexpected charges at 34% of wireless subscribers. Source: FCC. Verified live

Lambrecht and Skiera (2006), in the Journal of Marketing Research, examined about 10,882 internet-access bills over five months and found that up to 46.4% of customers showed flat-rate bias, choosing a tariff that was too large, against at most 5.8% who chose one that was too small. More than half of the flat-rate-biased group paid at least 100% more than the cheapest available tariff would have cost them. Customers on too-large plans did not churn more and held 87–135% higher lifetime value, while customers on too-small plans, the group that hits caps and pays overages, churned at monthly rates 340–1,040% higher and held slightly negative lifetime value. A related literature summary puts the share of flat-rate internet subscribers who would have saved money on pay-per-use at about 48%. Source: literature summary in the Journal of Revenue and Pricing Management. Index

DellaVigna and Malmendier (2006), in the American Economic Review, studied 7,752 US health-club members and found that members on flat monthly contracts above $70 attended 4.3 times a month and paid more than $17 per visit when a $10 per-visit pass existed, about 70% more per visit. 80% of monthly members would have saved money paying per visit, and average forgone savings ran to about $600 per membership. Buyers over-buy allowance because they choose for their expected peak usage rather than their average usage. Source: literature summary in the Journal of Revenue and Pricing Management. Index

Two smaller findings complete the picture. Spotify Premium includes 15 hours a month of audiobooks with paid top-ups beyond that allowance, and management said on the Q3 2023 call that some users were already buying extra hours on the first day while describing the base as very small, with no take-up rate disclosed. In Chargebee's 2025 survey, 70% of consumers said they are open to usage-based pricing, which records an attitude rather than behavior at a cap. Index

10

Assumptions

  • The usage shape anchors (median 20, p90 200, p99 1,500 actions per user per month) are analyst assumptions with no published source.
  • Task token counts, credit prices, and mix shares are estimates set by the analyst.
  • Text tasks split 60% input tokens and 40% output tokens.
  • An abandoned build costs the same as a kept build. No refund and no partial credit.
  • Top-up buyers cover exactly half of any shortfall.
  • In the margin simulation, users keep their plan, do not churn, and do not time usage to the billing cycle. Movement between plans is estimated separately, in the behavior layer.
  • Behavior defaults from published evidence where it exists (§09): elasticity −0.1, wall churn 13%, wall downgrades 8%. Paid take-up (20%), the upgrade share (15%), the paid-policy scalings, and the 0.35 price-response damper are analyst assumptions.
  • Users sort into plans by demand level: the lightest quarter of the population starts on Basic and the heaviest quarter on Advanced, so each plan's users form one band of the pooled usage distribution (Methodology §04).
  • Prices are list prices. No negotiated rates, prompt caching, or batch discounts.
  • All AI cost is charged against the full plan price, including the part of the price that pays for the website itself.
11

Limits

  • Overage revenue is an upper bound: it bills the full simulated shortfall. Top-up mode bills half of any shortfall, the half users are assumed to buy; the other half is forgone. Real heavy users upgrade, churn, or stop before paying several times the plan price.
  • Behavior defaults come from adjacent products, not this one: elasticity from $10–$20 entertainment subscriptions (far lower switching costs than a business website, so likely overstated here), wall churn from internet tariffs. Outputs show what follows from the rates chosen, not what will happen.
  • Paid take-up has no published value, and no published cap-hit split exists. Answers depending on the take-up slider rest on an unsettled assumption.
  • The equal 10,000-per-plan starting mix is a convention, not an observed mix: §09 filings show revenue concentrating in lower plan families.
  • Movement happens once, in month six. Downgraders are not re-tested against the lower plan's wall, and nobody is won back.
  • Sorting is perfect and the bands are equal quarters. Plan membership follows the persistent demand level exactly, so the model's bands are cleaner than real plan choice, which also reflects price sensitivity, team size, and habit. The 0.7 variance split between the persistent level and monthly noise is an analyst assumption with no published value: a lower split blurs the bands toward a single shared shape, and a higher one sharpens them. A user's level also never drifts across the six months.
  • One elasticity serves all four plans; competitor pull-in is not modelled.
  • Take-up restates revenue only. Serving cost per remaining user is held at the simulated figure, so adjusted margins under the paid policies are approximate. The take-up assumption itself is large: at the baseline plan settings it moves adjusted revenue per starting user by about $12 in top-up mode and about $44 in overage mode across the 10% to 25% sensitivity range. The unrestated-cost asymmetry is the smaller effect inside that swing, because serving cost per user is an order of magnitude below plan prices in this model, but it is not quantified separately and the adjusted margins should be read as approximate for that reason.
  • The four bands are quarters of one fitted lognormal, so the four shapes are slices of a single assumed curve rather than four measured distributions. If real per-plan demand differs in spread as well as in centre, the band tails here are wrong in shape, not only in level.
  • Mix averages hide variation. Users with the same action count are costed identically.
  • There is no demand response. A larger allowance does not change usage in the model. In a product, it would.
  • The inputs are snapshots. Plan prices and provider rates date from October and November 2026 and will drift.
  • The margin simulation says nothing about willingness to pay, conversion, or churn; it covers cost and margin only. Movement is estimated separately in the behavior layer, on the evidence in §09, and inherits that evidence's limits.
  • The behavior layer's wall split is reconciled by construction: the raw test total (price departures plus wall churn, Basic downgrades counted) is capped at twice the 4.2% baseline churn, and wall churn scales down to fit when the raw responses exceed the cap. At the defaults, realized departures run above the cap figure because Basic downgrades are counted in the test but not scaled; under a net price inflow they can land below it, because price departures enter the test floored at zero. Read adjusted figures as bounded by that discipline rule, not as a forecast of actual departures.
  • Upgraders are costed at the average cost of the tier they join, though they are the heaviest users of the tier they left. The upgrade flow's cost side is therefore understated and the adjusted margin flattered by an amount the model does not separately quantify.
  • Image generation scores 100 on the quality index by construction: it is a flat per-image cost with no routing decision, so a fifth of the action mix sits at frontier quality in the index regardless of the routing settings.
