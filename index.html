07

Competitor packaging

Three alternatives to the Squarespace shape, from public pricing and help pages checked in October and November 2026. Each answers the same questions differently: what burns credits, what happens at zero, and whether you can buy more.

Framer

Published burn rates, per-model pricing

  • A full AI landing page costs about 300 credits. The burn rate is published, so users can budget before they click.
  • Model choice is a price multiplier: 0.9× / 1× / 1.8× across model tiers. The routing decision sits with the user.
  • Squarespace's build, at 25 credits, hides the same choice inside one blended rate.

Wix

Generation unmetered, agents metered

  • Site generation is unmetered. Credits apply only to agentic tools that run after the site exists.
  • No rollover and no top-ups. More capacity means upgrading the plan.
  • In this simulator that is the hard cap with top-ups off. Margin is protected by construction, and heavy users become upgrade leads.

Webflow

Pooled workspace credits, cheap refills

  • Credits are pooled at the workspace, not per site. One busy site can draw on the whole pool.
  • Refill packs run 2,000 credits for $20, one cent per credit, well under any plan's implied rate in section 1.
  • That only works if a Webflow credit buys far less compute than a credit here. Credit counts do not compare across vendors. Cost per credit does.

02

How to read this table

Static against adjusted

Portfolio margin and mean cost hold the starting population fixed. The adjusted columns let it move under the behavior settings: users churn at the wall, downgrade one plan, or pay to keep going. A policy that raises the static margin while lowering adjusted revenue per starting user is trading customers for margin, and the two column groups are where that trade becomes visible. Overage revenue is a mechanical upper bound in both groups: it bills the full simulated shortfall before any upgrade, churn, or spending cap.

Why the margin column moves least

The model charges all AI cost against the full plan price, including the part of the price that pays for the website itself, so portfolio margins sit near the ceiling in every preset and the spread between them is a few points. The presets move the other outcomes by much more: mean serving cost per user runs from a few cents under all-standard routing to several dollars under overage, adjusted revenue per starting user spreads by tens of dollars, and the ending population spreads by thousands of users. Read the table on those columns first and treat the margin column as the guardrail check.

Deltas and shading

Every delta is read against Baseline, the published plans as they stand. The shaded cell in each column is the best value in that column across all rows, including your current settings. The highlighted row is the setup currently loaded in the simulator; a Custom setup joins the table as its own row as soon as a control moves.

Where the settings live

The presets themselves are chosen in the simulator. The behavior rates behind the adjusted columns are set in the simulator's policy section (§03), and their evidence and derivations are in the Methodology (§08 and §09).

Simulator Comparison Methodology

Documentation

Methodology

How the simulator works, where its defaults come from, and where it stops being useful. This page describes the code on the simulator page. If the two disagree, the code is right and this page is out of date.

01

What the model does

The simulator prices one question: what does it cost to serve AI usage that is sold as plan credits, and which allowance and shortfall rules keep that cost inside a target margin?

It is a pricing model, not a forecast. It holds no data from Squarespace's actual business.

02

Inputs and defaults

Plans

Squarespace's published tiers at annual billing, checked October 2026. Basic is $19 a month with 10 credits granted once and no monthly refill. Core is $29 with 20 credits a month. Plus is $49 with 40 a month. Advanced is $99 with 120 a month. Prices in the model are monthly equivalents.

Provider rate card

Public list prices per 1M tokens, November 2026. The frontier model (Sonnet class) is $3 input and $15 output. The standard model (mini and Haiku class) is $0.30 input and $1.50 output. Image generation is a flat $0.04 per image. A blended text rate assumes 60% input tokens and 40% output tokens. The effective rate is the routing-weighted average of the two blended rates. Routing defaults to 30% of text tasks on the frontier model.

Tasks

Five tasks carry demand: site build, page regeneration, copy draft, image generation, and SEO scan. Each has a token count, a credit price, and a share of monthly actions. These are analyst estimates, not published figures, and they are the softest inputs in the model.

Competitor benchmarks

The packaging notes come from Framer, Wix, and Webflow public pricing and help pages, checked in October and November 2026. They describe packaging only. They do not feed the simulation.

Behavior evidence

The behavior defaults come from a review of the published record completed in November 2026 and reproduced in §09 of this Methodology. Its main sources are Antenna data on Netflix reported by TechCrunch in 2022, Spotify's Q3 2023 earnings call, ChartMogul churn data by revenue per account as reproduced by GrowPanel, Recurly's 2023 churn benchmarks, Chargebee's 2025 Global Consumer Insights survey, and two academic papers, Grubb and Osborne (2015) and Lambrecht and Skiera (2006). Each figure in §09 is tagged by how it was checked. Figures tagged as index-grade rest on search-result text or a secondary reproduction rather than a page fetched and read during the review, and the defaults that rest on them carry that status with them.

03

Usage distribution

Monthly actions per user follow a lognormal distribution. The median slider sets the centre exactly. The spread (sigma) is fitted to the p90 and p99 sliders by least squares on the log scale, with each point weighted by its z-score: 1.2816 for p90 and 2.3263 for p99.

One lognormal usually cannot pass through all three points, so the fitted p90 and p99 differ from the slider values. The simulator prints both. The size of the gap says how well a lognormal describes the three points chosen.

A user's credit demand is their action count multiplied by the average credits per action across the task mix. Serving cost is the action count multiplied by the average cost per action. Individual actions are not drawn one at a time. That keeps the run fast and the results stable. It also means two users with the same action count are costed identically, whatever tasks they actually ran.

04

Simulation design

The simulation is a Monte Carlo with a fixed seed. A mulberry32 generator produces the same 10,000 standard normal draws for any plan and any setting. Identical settings therefore give identical figures, and a change in the output comes from a change in the inputs, not from fresh randomness.

Each user is simulated for six months so rollover banks settle into a pattern. All reported figures are month six. Costs at the median, p90, and p99 are read from the simulated users nearest those demand percentiles, not from the fitted curve.

05

Policy mechanics

Hard cap

Actions stop when the credit balance runs out. Unserved demand costs nothing and earns nothing.

Overage billing

Full demand is served. Credits beyond the balance are billed at the plan's implied rate (price divided by monthly credits) times the overage multiple. Basic has no implied rate, so its overage is priced at the top-up price.

Top-up packs

Half of any shortfall, in credits and in cost, is bought at the top-up price. The other half is forgone: the user does fewer actions. The half is an assumption, not a measurement, and the top-up results are sensitive to it.

Rollover and breakage

With rollover on, unused credits bank into the next month up to one month of allowance. Anything above the cap expires. Basic's one-time grant lands in month one only. Breakage is expired credits divided by credits available in month six: the month's grant plus the banked balance.

06

Margins and the picker

Margin is per user per month: (revenue minus serving cost) divided by revenue. Revenue is the plan price plus any overage or top-up revenue. A tier breaches the guardrail when its overall margin, or the margin on its p99 user, falls below the floor set in the simulator.

Portfolio figures average the four plans with equal user counts on each plan. The allowance picker holds price and policy constant and swaps one plan's allowance for a chosen percentile of simulated credit demand. It answers a narrow question: what that allowance, on its own, does to breakage and margin.

07

Decomposition

The decomposition starts from portfolio profit per user under the current settings, then applies each shock on its own. Rate: provider prices change and nothing else moves. Mix: the routing share changes at unchanged prices. Volume: a user's action count scales by the growth factor. A fourth run applies all three together.

Interaction is what the combined change leaves after the three single effects are subtracted. It is a residual, not a measured quantity. Under a hard cap the volume effect is near zero by construction, because serving cost is bounded by allowances. Read that as a property of the policy, not as evidence that growth is free.

08

Buyer behavior

The margin simulation holds the population fixed: 10,000 users per plan, whatever the prices and rules. The behavior layer sits on top of it and lets the population move. It runs in two steps, in month six only, and it does not feed back into the margin figures in section 4 of the simulator. It produces its own adjusted revenue and margin, shown next to the static ones.

The defaults in this layer are set from published evidence checked in November 2026, and §09 of this Methodology carries the figures, the sources, and the caveats in full. The evidence base has a clear shape. No published, traceable price-elasticity coefficient for SaaS exists from any named firm, and no public source reports how Squarespace's users react to the credit wall, because Squarespace does not publish upgrade, downgrade, or churn rates by plan. The defaults therefore combine the closest citable evidence: consumer subscription price events for elasticity, billing-platform churn levels for the baseline, a survey ratio for downgrades against cancellations, and an academic finding on churn among users whose plan is too small for their usage. One rate, paid take-up at the wall, has no published value anywhere in the record found, and it is kept as an explicit assumption with a flagged sensitivity range.

Price response

Each plan's price is read against its published price. A plan's share shifts by its relative price change raised to the elasticity, which defaults to −0.1. That default is the short-run churn elasticity the evidence review derived from the Netflix increase of January 2022, where Antenna data put monthly churn at 3.0% in the increase month, up 0.95 points from a 2.0% average in 2021, and 3.3% by March after a $1–$2 increase on all tiers. Spotify's 10% increase in July 2023 produced no detectable churn change, which places the lower bound between 0 and −0.1, and the 2011 Netflix case reported in OpenStax gives a realised arc elasticity of about −0.07. All three are consumer entertainment products priced at $10–$20 with near-zero switching costs, so the default reads as an upper bound on sensitivity for a product that hosts a business's website, domain, and search presence. Plans that got cheaper relative to the others gain share, and plans that got dearer lose it. About a third of the response is treated as leaving the product entirely, applied through the average price change across the four plans, and the rest is switching between plans. That one-third split is a modeling choice with no published source. Users who arrive because prices fell are added the same way, so a price cut can end with more users than it started with.

The wall

The share of users who hit the wall on each plan comes from the simulation itself: one minus the share whose demand fits inside the available credits in month six. The published check on that share is Grubb and Osborne (2015), who found that about 20% of users on $35–$55 cellular plans incurred overage fees in a period. Those users split four ways. A share cancels, a share drops one plan, a share pays to keep going where the policy allows it, and the rest stay and accept the limit. Churn at the wall defaults to 13% under a hard cap. The figure is derived rather than measured: ChartMogul's platform data put median monthly churn at 4.2% for subscriptions at $25–$100 per account, and Lambrecht and Skiera (2006) found that customers on a plan too small for their usage churned at monthly rates 3.4 to 10.4 times the rate of other customers, so the default applies a conservative 3× stand-in to the baseline and rounds the resulting 12.6% to 13%. Downgrade at the wall defaults to 8%, from the downgrade-to-cancel ratio in Chargebee's 2025 Global Consumer Insights survey, where 14% of consumers who faced an increase downgraded and 22% cancelled some subscriptions. The churn and downgrade rates are set for a hard cap and then scaled down for the paid policies, with churn at 40% of the hard cap rate under overage billing and 55% under top-up packs, and downgrades at 50% and 60%. Those four scalings are modeling assumptions with no published source, retained so a reader can test how much a paid way through the wall would have to soften the response before it changes the answer. Basic has no plan below it, so its downgraders are counted as churn.

Paid take-up

Take-up is the share of wall users who pay. It defaults to 20%. No published study or vendor report found reports this rate for any SaaS product, so the default is an assumption placed inside the 10–25% sensitivity range the evidence review suggests modeling, and it should be read that way rather than as a sourced parameter. Under a hard cap it is zero, because there is nothing to buy. The static overage figures bill the full shortfall, and the static top-up figures assume half of it is bought. The behavior layer restates that revenue at the take-up rate instead: overage revenue is scaled by the rate, and top-up revenue is scaled against the half already assumed. Serving cost per remaining user is not restated. That is a simplification, and it is stated as one in the limits.

Adjusted totals

Users who leave take their revenue and their cost with them. Users who downgrade are counted at the lower plan's price and cost from that point. Adjusted revenue is the total across the ending population. Adjusted margin is that revenue minus the ending population's serving cost, divided by the revenue. Comparing adjusted revenue per starting user against the static figure is what turns a margin gain into a revenue loss when a policy change pushes people out.

Source: Wix fiscal 2023 results, February 21, 2024. nasdaq.com Verified live

Two consumer analogues bound the shape of a tier mix. After Netflix phased out its mid-priced ad-free Basic tier for new and rejoining members, its cheapest ads tier grew about 70% quarter over quarter for two consecutive quarters and took 40% of all new sign-ups in ads markets, according to the Q4 2023 shareholder letter. That shift was engineered by removing a tier rather than by a pure price change. Source: sec.gov Verified live At Spotify in Q3 2023, 226 million Premium subscribers against 574 million total monthly users means 39% of users pay and 61% stay free after a decade of freemium operation.

The category's current price ladders, taken from October 2026 checked-pricing compilations, clear the market inside the same band this simulator models: Squarespace at $19, $29, $49, and $99 a month billed annually and $25 to $139 billed monthly; Wix at $17, $29, $39, and $159; Shopify at $29, $79, and $299 with Plus from $2,300; and GoDaddy entry pricing at about $9.99. A live check of Squarespace's own pricing page was attempted during the review and the browser failed, so these ladder figures are index-grade. Sources: Rankite and Wix. Index

Outside options bound willingness to pay from above. A do-it-yourself builder build runs $0 to $450 on Forbes Advisor's figures, a freelancer-built basic site runs about $6,760 all-in for year one on FreshBooks' breakdown, and most agency projects reviewed on Clutch cost under $10,000, all as compiled in Zarla's 2026 cost guide. A $19–$99 monthly plan, $228 to $1,188 a year, sits an order of magnitude below the freelancer and agency alternatives, which is consistent with the low price sensitivity observed inside this band, although no published study converts that relationship into a sensitivity coefficient. Source: Zarla. Index

On billing period, the modal annual discount across more than 1,000 SaaS businesses in Recurly's plan-structure study from January 2017 to July 2018 was 16.7%, or two months free, while Squarespace's own ladder embeds an annual discount of about 24% at Basic. Allowance and overage behavior, from the academic record

The strongest evidence on what happens at an allowance limit comes from metered telecom and internet tariffs rather than from SaaS, and the review found no published split for any SaaS product. The components that are published point in one direction: the risk sits with users on a plan that is too small for them, and it expresses itself as churn rather than as willing overage payment.

Grubb and Osborne (2015), in the American Economic Review, studied US cellular bills from 2002 to 2004 and found that about 20% of users on $35–$55 monthly plans incurred overage fees of about $20 a month. Across the sample period, 19% of subscribers incurred an overage above $100, 8% above $200, and 3% above $300. Consumers were overconfident about how much their usage would vary, that overconfidence cost them $76 a year at 2002–04 prices, and users who received usage alerts reduced their usage significantly. The one-in-five incidence rate is the best published anchor for the share of allowance-plan users who hit a cap in a period. Sources: American Economic Association and the University of Toronto summary. Verified live

An FCC consumer survey from 2010 with 3,005 US adults found that 17% of mobile users, about one in six or roughly 30 million people, had experienced a sudden bill increase that was not caused by a plan change, and 84% of them said their carrier did not contact them as they approached or exceeded their allowance. More than a third of those users saw a jump of at least $50 and 23% saw at least $100. A GAO figure from 2009 cited in the same proceeding put unexpected charges at 34% of wireless subscribers. Source: FCC. Verified live

Lambrecht and Skiera (2006), in the Journal of Marketing Research, examined about 10,882 internet-access bills over five months and found that up to 46.4% of customers showed flat-rate bias, choosing a tariff that was too large, against at most 5.8% who chose one that was too small. More than half of the flat-rate-biased group paid at least 100% more than the cheapest available tariff would have cost them. Customers on too-large plans did not churn more and carried 87–135% higher lifetime value, while customers on too-small plans, the group that hits caps and pays overages, churned at monthly rates 340–1,040% higher and carried slightly negative lifetime value. A related literature summary puts the share of flat-rate internet subscribers who would have saved money on pay-per-use at about 48%. Source: literature summary in the Journal of Revenue and Pricing Management. Index

DellaVigna and Malmendier (2006), in the American Economic Review, studied 7,752 US health-club members and found that members on flat monthly contracts above $70 attended 4.3 times a month and paid more than $17 per visit when a $10 per-visit pass existed, about 70% more per visit. 80% of monthly members would have saved money paying per visit, and average forgone savings ran to about $600 per membership. Buyers over-buy allowance because they choose for their expected peak usage rather than their average usage. Index

Two smaller findings complete the picture. Spotify Premium includes 15 hours a month of audiobooks with paid top-ups beyond that allowance, and management said on the Q3 2023 call that some users were already buying extra hours on the first day while describing the base as very small, with no take-up rate disclosed. In Chargebee's 2025 survey, 70% of consumers said they are open to usage-based pricing, which records an attitude rather than behavior at a cap. Index

Open questions, stated plainly. The published record found in November 2026 does not answer the following, and this simulator does not pretend otherwise.
  • No SaaS price-elasticity coefficient from ProfitWell or Paddle, OpenView, KeyBanc, Stripe, Zuora, Recurly, or Chargebee, and no academic elasticity estimate for subscription software, was found. Assumptions

  • Task token counts, credit prices, and mix shares are estimates set by the analyst.
  • Text tasks split 60% input tokens and 40% output tokens.
  • An abandoned build costs the same as a kept build. No refund and no partial credit.
  • Top-up buyers cover exactly half of any shortfall.
  • In the margin simulation, users keep their plan, do not churn, and do not time usage to the billing cycle. Movement between plans is estimated separately, in the behavior layer.
  • Behavior defaults are set from published evidence where the record provides it: price elasticity of −0.1 from consumer subscription price events reported by Antenna and Spotify, hard-cap wall churn of 13% derived from ChartMogul's 4.2% baseline monthly churn at $25–$100 and Lambrecht and Skiera's too-small-plan churn multiple, and wall downgrades of 8% from Chargebee's downgrade-to-cancel ratio of about 0.6. Paid take-up of 20% where a paid option exists has no published value and remains an analyst assumption inside a 10–25% sensitivity range. The scalings that soften wall churn and downgrades under overage billing and top-up packs, and the one-third share of the price response treated as leaving the product, are also analyst assumptions.
  • Users on all four plans share one usage distribution.
  • Prices are list prices. No negotiated rates, prompt caching, or batch discounts.
  • All AI cost is charged against the full plan price, including the part of the price that pays for the website itself.
11

Limits

  • Overage and top-up revenue is an upper bound. It bills the full simulated shortfall. Real heavy users upgrade, churn, or stop before paying several times the plan price.
  • The behavior defaults are anchored in published evidence from adjacent products rather than measured on this one. The elasticity comes from $10–$20 consumer entertainment subscriptions whose switching costs are far lower than a business website's, so it likely overstates how strongly this category responds, and the wall churn multiple comes from internet access tariffs rather than software credits. The vendor's real elasticity and wall reactions are not public, so the behavior outputs show what follows from the rates chosen, not what will happen. A reader who sets different rates gets different answers, by design.
  • Paid take-up at the wall has no published value for any SaaS product in the record searched, and no published split of users who hit a usage cap into pay, upgrade, reduce, or churn exists. Any answer that depends on the take-up slider depends on an assumption the published record cannot settle.
  • No builder publishes subscriber counts by plan tier, so the equal starting mix of 10,000 users per plan is a modeling convention rather than an observed mix. The filings in §09 show that real revenue concentrates in the lower plan families, with Squarespace's Presence family at 69.6% of fiscal 2023 revenue.
  • Movement happens once, in month six. A user who downgrades is not tested against the lower plan's wall in the same step, and nobody is won back in later months.
  • Price response uses one elasticity for all four plans. In practice the cheapest and most expensive plans rarely respond at the same rate, and a price change on one plan can pull in users from competitors, which the model does not represent.
  • Take-up restates revenue only. Serving cost per remaining user is held at the simulated figure, so adjusted margins under the paid policies are approximate.
  • One distribution serves all four tiers. Higher tiers attract heavier users in practice, so costs at the top are likely understated and costs at the bottom overstated.
  • Mix averages hide variation. Users with the same action count are costed identically.
  • There is no demand response. A larger allowance does not change usage in the model. In a product, it would.
  • The inputs are snapshots. Plan prices and provider rates date from October and November 2026 and will drift.
  • The model says nothing about willingness to pay, conversion, or churn. It covers cost and margin only.
